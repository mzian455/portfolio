  1. Autonomous Intelligent Machines builds a team of small robots that play soccer on their own. I work on how they move and how they decide.

    Motion planning and control. I use the grSim simulator to measure how the robots respond to movement commands and how accurately they follow a planned path. I have compared PID against PID with feedforward, tested whether an extended Kalman filter improves tracking when the camera data is noisy, and studied when a robot should replan to avoid a collision.

    Reinforcement learning for tactics. I am researching how to design rewards that teach robots when to reposition, pass or shoot, and comparing single-objective with multi-objective learning. One option is a hybrid: a tactical layer weighs scoring chances against defensive risk, and a motion layer executes the chosen action reliably.

    These experiments decide what the team implements and what gets validated on the physical robots. Four of them are rebuilt in the Lab below.

  2. WayLine helps a planner decide where a pipeline should go before anyone commits to a route.

    1. The planner picks a start point and an end point.
    2. WayLine generates candidate routes from terrain, geology, soil, land-use and existing-infrastructure data.
    3. Each route is scored on 6+ constraint categories and on 5 regulatory dimensions: permits, regulators, consultation requirements, approval timelines and regulatory risk.
    4. The routes are compared on an interactive 3D map by construction cost, engineering feasibility and regulatory complexity.

    The idea is regulation-first routing. A shorter route can cost less to build but cross land that triggers extra approvals, and WayLine surfaces that trade-off while the route is still being chosen.

  3. Drone operators juggle radios, controllers, screens and maps at once. Voice2Vector lets an operator say what they want, such as "take off to 10 metres" or "fly to the northwest watch tower", and keeps their eyes up.

    1. Listen. Speech is captured in the browser and transcribed to text.
    2. Parse. A fast rule-based parser handles standard phrasing. Ambiguous commands fall through to a language model that extracts the intent.
    3. Validate. Every command is checked against the compound layout, no-go zones, altitude limits and what the drone can do. Unsafe commands are rejected with an explanation.
    4. Execute. Valid commands are sent as MAVLink messages to an ArduPilot flight controller, which flies the drone in a Gazebo physics simulation.
    5. Report. The operator sees the result on a 2D map and a 3D view, with every command logged for review.

    A small version of the parse and validate steps runs in the Lab below.

  4. Energy companies have to prove they find and repair leaks. Auditors spend hours checking PDF reports by hand against regulatory directives such as D060, D071 and D056. AudItron reads the report, cites the exact rule, and coaches the auditor through each finding.

    1. Upload a PDF audit report and choose which directives apply.
    2. Review. The system flags compliance issues as red, yellow or green. Each one links to the exact regulatory clause and is highlighted on the page of the PDF it came from.
    3. Coach. A voice-first coach asks questions about each finding, so the auditor builds understanding and not dependence.
    4. Document. Every decision is logged with who made it, when and why, which leaves a trail a regulator can follow.

    The hard part was stopping the model from inventing citations, because compliance has no tolerance for that. Every model output has to match a strict schema, and a verification layer rejects any finding that is not grounded in a regulation passage the system actually retrieved.

    Under the hood the directives are split into chunks, embedded as vectors, and searched with a hybrid of keyword and vector search. Long evaluations run as background jobs.

  5. Nurses are interrupted constantly, often in the middle of giving medication. Coming back and thinking "did I already give this dose?" is stressful and unsafe. Checkpoint lets a nurse save what they were doing and restore it later.

    1. Capture. One large button records a short phrase, for example "Patel, room 204, metoprolol 25 milligrams, bed alarm".
    2. Structure. The recording is transcribed, and a model extracts the patient, room, medication, dose and notes as structured fields.
    3. Label. Each field is marked confirmed, uncertain or missing, so uncertainty is shown and not hidden.
    4. Resume. A resume screen shows who the patient is, what was being given, what the nurse said, and how long ago.

    Saved checkpoints sit in an unfinished-task queue, and interruptions are logged to a dashboard that shows when and how they cluster. The app also scans patient wristband barcodes. It was my first iOS app.
